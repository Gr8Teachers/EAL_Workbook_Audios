Sentence stress patterns

In English, speakers stress different words in a sentence. This means we say some words louder, longer and at a higher pitch and other words more quietly, quicker and at a lower pitch.

The words we stress in a sentence are called content words. The words we do not stress are called function words and usually have weak stress.

The stressed words provide the information or main content and help the listener to hear the important information. The unstressed words include the articles, conjunctions, and prepositions. The unstressed words need to be spoken clearly and quickly but not loudly.

Content and function words

Content words are the most important words in a sentence. Content words carry meaning. These are usually nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs. You need to hear these words to understand the meaning. Other important content words are yes and no. However, function words are grammar words; they do not carry meaning. Function words include articles, prepositions and linking words. We can still understand the meaning of the sentence without function words, but we need them to join the words grammatically.

Here are types of content words that are stressed, with some examples.

Types of content words Examples
nouns chair, shop
main verb call, go
adjectives and adverbs happy, well
question words what, when, where, how, why
contractions with not can’t, isn’t
other key words yes, no, this, that, those

Here are function words that have a weak stress:

Types of function words Examples
preposition to, for
the verb to be is, are, was
auxiliary verbs can, does
articles the, an
conjunctions and, so, but
personal pronouns you, she, we, her, him, our

It is also important to know that the rules for sentence stress are not always the same. Usually, content words are stressed, and function words are not. However, we do not stress all content words. As a speaker, we can choose which words we want to stress. We could in fact even stress some function words. We have flexibility in the words we stress.

One reason for flexibility in sentence stress is to show importance.

For example, look at this sentence:

I think you’re right.

Now look at the different ways we can use sentence stress to show different meaning and importance.

Sentence stress Meaning
I think you’re right. General agreement.
I think you’re right. I agree with you, but not with other people.
I think you’re right. I agree, but I still have doubts.
I think you’re right. I agree with you even if others don’t.

In this example, we can see that the speaker can choose to stress the content words or the function words. It depends on the situation and the meaning you want to show.

It is important to be aware that when we stress words, we do not shout or raise our voices. If we do this, then it would be impolite.

Another reason for stress flexibility is to show a contrast. For example:

A: Beijing is the capital of France.

B: No, Beijing is the capital of China.

She doesn’t live in Parramatta; she lives in Pendle Hill.

Types of sentence stress

There are different types of sentence stresses:

  • tonic stress

  • emphatic stress

  • contrastive stress

  • new information stress.

Types of sentence stresses Description Example sentence
Tonic stress It emphasises the content words in an utterance. I am waiting for my sister at the park.
Emphatic stress It communicates how extraordinary something is. That was absolutely terrifying!
Contrastive stress It changes the meaning of the sentence.

Zoe walked to school. (It was Zoe who walked, not someone else.)

Zoe walked to school. (Zoe walked, rather than drove or rode the train.)

Zoe walked to school. (Zoe walked to school, not somewhere else.)

Zoe walked to school yesterday. (Zoe walked yesterday, not today or some other time.)
New information stress It emphasises new information. I like coffee, not tea.

Show transcript – Job seeking process

Wendy: Good morning, everyone! My name is Wendy, and I'm a college vocational counselor. Today I'm going to talk about preparing yourself for the job seeking process.

Now, is anyone in the class working already? 

Audience: Yeah.

Wendy: OK. Some of you have found a job. What sort of work are you doing?

Man: Weekend, part-time. I'm stacking shelves in a supermaket.

Wendy: OK. And how did you find that job?

Man: My brother-in-law works there.

Wendy: OK, so you used your contacts. One of the most common ways of finding a job. I'll come back to that point later. Now, in this morning's talk, I'll cover a number of issues. Firstly, where job seeking courses are held. And then the idea of networking, followed by barriers to finding a job. And finally, how to overcome these barriers. Oh, by the way, stop me if at any time there's something you don't understand.
