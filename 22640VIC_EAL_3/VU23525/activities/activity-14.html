Positive tone

A positive tone means the speaker is positive, friendly, personal or chatty. They use words that make others feel happy and valued.

  • Well done on your presentation! It was really interesting!

  • If you need any help, just let me know. I’m here for you!

  • I’m impressed with your dedication and effort.

Attentive interested rising

If the tone stress is said with an extra pitch, we know the speaker is asking for something to be repeated or clarified. They might also be showing that they can’t believe what they are hearing.

  • Person A: I’m taking up TAXidermy this autumn.

  • Person B: Taking up WHAT? (clarification)

  • Person A: She passed her DRIving test.

  • Person B: She PASSED? (disbelief)

Negative tone falling

A negative tone falling means the speaker sounds negative, critical, impersonal or bossy. They use words that can make others feel bad or unappreciated.

  • This is not good enough.

  • This needs a lot of improvement.

  • I’m disappointed with the results.

Bored flat intonation

Bored flat intonation means the speaker sounds uninterested and not excited. When someone talks this way, their voice stays the same and doesn't show much emotion or change in tone.

  • I guess we could do that.

  • Yeah, whatever you want.

  • I don’t really care.

Neutral-flat

Neutral-flat intonation means the speaker sounds calm and without emotion. When someone talks this way, their voice stays steady and even without showing strong feelings or excitement.

  • The meeting is at 10 am.

  • You can leave your documents on my desk.

  • Please submit your report by Friday.

Sad falling

Sad falling intonation means the speaker sounds unhappy or disappointed. When someone talks this way, their voice drops at the end of sentences, showing their sadness or discouragement.

  • I didn’t get the job.

  • I’m feeling really down today.

  • It wasn’t what I expected.

Surprised

Surprised intonation means the speaker sounds amazed or shocked. When someone talks this way, their voice rises at the end of sentences, showing their surprise or disbelief.

  • Really? That’s amazing!

  • I can’t believe it!

  • You did what?

Uncertain

Uncertain intonation means the speaker sounds unsure or hesitant. When someone talks this way, their voice rises slightly at the end of sentences, showing their doubt or lack of confidence.

  • Are you sure this is right?

  • I’m not certain about this.

  • I think it’s correct?

Show transcript – Radio Interview: Healthy Lifestyles

Richard: Now for our new segment which we call Better, Longer because it’s all about healthy living and how we can live better and live longer. Today I’d like to welcome back to the program Anna Novak from the Out there program at the Department of Health.

Anna: Thank you, Richard.

Richard: Now today you’re going to talk to us about food. And you’re going to tell us we all eat the wrong things and we all eat too much.

Anna: No, no, no, not at all. There are some people certainly who eat too much of the wrong food but most of us have pretty sensible diets.

Richard: Ah, the sensible diet! Now what is that? Because you see, everybody seems to have a different idea about what’s sensible and what’s not.

Anna: Well, when it comes to a sensible diet, we can divide food into 4 main groups. First of all there are the grains and we should eat plenty of grains like rice, bread and pasta. Next, lots of fruit and vegetables. After that comes the protein foods like meat, cheese and other dairy food. Many Australians eat far too much meat and cheese. Fish is in this group too, but most people don’t eat enough fish. Fish is good for the heart and other things too. And finally, we should eat as little as possible of fatty food, of sugar and salt. We know that people who eat a lot of fat become overweight and develop heart problems.

Richard: All the yummy stuff!

Anna: Well, I think the good stuff is yummy too, Richard.

Richard: But do I have to give up my junk food? No more chips!

Anna: Richard, the good news is you don’t have to give up anything. You can have your chips, people can have their hamburger or their pizza or their meat pie, but just not too often. If you like junk food, have it, but it should be a treat, something you have no more than once a week.

Richard: I could live with that.

Anna: Most people can. And they’ll live healthier and longer!

Richard: Sounds good. Anna, thanks for coming in.

Anna: A pleasure Richard.

Richard: That was Anna Novak from the Department of Health.
