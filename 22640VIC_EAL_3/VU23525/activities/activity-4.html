Key features of style and structure

When you listen to a presentation, it is important to understand the style and structure.

Style

Formal presentations are those when the presenter is an expert, and the listeners are learners. Formal presentations typically occur live in further education, such as TAFE, online, or in the workplace. The style of a formal presentation is well-structured and delivered in a well-sequenced and logical order. The formal presentation is well structured because the speaker has time to read, listen and understand the topic well before speaking. Simply, the speaker has time to prepare and get ready. They have time to gather their information and write a plan. This preparation and organisation are necessary because the presenter wants the listener to learn and understand the information. Often, there is little interaction between the presenter and the audience, so it is not interactive.

Casual presentations are those given by people who know each other well, such as work colleagues, sporting club members or friends. They may be spoken in an informal setting such as a staff lunchroom, a sports club meeting room or a restaurant. In these settings, the speaker wants to create a more comfortable atmosphere. Also, the speaker may have less time to prepare their speech. Therefore, the style of a casual presentation is more informal, less organised and less structured. It may include questions from the speaker or audience and can be more engaging and interactive.

Structure

The structure of a formal presentation usually follows a predictable format.

  • Introduction – The speaker usually starts with a formal introduction. This includes a greeting, self-introduction, the purpose of the presentation and an overview of the topic.

  • Main ideas – The body of the presentation includes the main ideas. Each idea is explained with details and examples. The speaker may use examples, statistics or quotes to support their main ideas. These help to explain and strengthen their arguments.

  • Conclusion – The speaker will end with a conclusion. This summarises the main points and may include information for further reading.

The structure of a casual presentation is relaxed and less organised. It can include more personal stories, some jokes humour and interaction with the audience.

  • Introduction – The speaker usually starts with a relaxed introduction, such as, ‘Hi or Hello, everyone; as you may know, I’m Jo.’ The overview may begin with a joke on the theme of the talk.

  • Main ideas – The main ideas in the body of the presentation should be planned; however, they are presented in a relaxed manner. The idea may begin with a personal insight or story relating to the idea. The speaker will include their personal feelings and use more pronouns and informal expressions. Often, there is interaction between the audience and the speaker, with questions and comments from the listeners.

  • Conclusion – The speaker may finish with some questions or follow up with a poem or song.

How to analyse a presentation

  1. Listen for the introduction:

    • Note the greeting and how the speaker introduces the topic.

    • Identify the outline of the presentation.

  2. Identify the main ideas:

    • Listen for signal words that tell you the sequence of points.

    • Take note of each main point and any details or examples given.

  3. Pay attention to the conclusion:

    • Hear how the speaker summarises the main points.

    • Notice any final messages or calls to action.

  4. Observe the style:

    • Determine if the style and structure are well organised or more freely flowing or disorganised.

Show transcript – Clean Up Australia presentation

Abdul: Good morning everyone. My name’s Abdul Latif and I’m a Clean Up Australia Day volunteer. I’m very pleased to be here today to talk to you about this event.
You may or may not have heard about Clean Up Australia. Does anyone know what happens on this day? No? Then this is a good opportunity for me to share with you and to invite you to join us in cleaning up your new home.
So what is Clean Up Australia? It’s a non-profit organisation founded by Ian Kiernan in 1989. Ian believes that simple, easy actions can help protect the health of our environment and we can all help to make a difference. So every year on the first Sunday in March thousands of Australians volunteer to clean up our local environment. We collect and remove rubbish. Take me for example, I joined Clean Up Australia last year and I worked with other volunteers to clear up our local creek. You wouldn’t believe what we found there: bottles, plastic bags, wallets, a shopping trolley and even an old fridge! Yep, I’m not kidding.
Did you know that Australians are among the highest producers of waste, per person, in the world? Shocking, isn’t it? And 80 million plastic bags are dumped in Australia each year. This creates a huge threat to our marine life. So how can you be a part of our clean up community? You, your family or friends can either come along and join us at a designated site or choose a spot in your local area. It could be a park, waterway or bushland and help clean the area up. If you want more information, go to our website. It’ll tell you where the existing clean up sites in your area are and you can also register online to be a volunteer or ring us on 1800 282 329. OK. Are there any questions? Yes, the student at the back.

Student: Ah yeah. Do you provide protective clothing?

Abdul: Ah .. no, we actually don’t, um .. so you should bring protective gloves, ah .. a hat and sunscreen of course, as it can get pretty hot in March. And it’s best to wear old clothes as you’ll probably get pretty dirty. Any more questions? No? Then I hope to see you on Clean Up Australia Day. If you have any more questions, please feel free to see me after this. Thank you very much for your time.
